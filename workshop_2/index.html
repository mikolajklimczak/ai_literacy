Briefing an AI, checking its work, and knowing what you tested

In Workshop 1 you saw that language models generate text from learned patterns and that different models often disagree on facts. In this session you will test how the content and structure of your input changes the output. You will take a realistic problem, diagnose why the first output fails, translate raw situation notes into a structured prompt across four documented rounds, and then verify the final answer yourself against the facts. A clearer prompt makes a good answer more likely. It does not make the answer correct.

Tools, privacy and assessment rules

  • Use one AI tool and one mode for all four rounds, so changes in output come from your prompt and not from a different model. The free version of Microsoft Copilot, ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Mistral Le Chat and similar tools are all fine.
  • Microsoft Copilot: sign in with your university account and check that the data-protection indicator is shown. If you are unsure what your licence includes, ask the IT office.
  • Free tiers change without notice. They may switch the underlying model, limit "thinking" modes or stop mid-session. Record what you actually used in the fields below.
  • Start a new, temporary chat with memory and personalisation switched off if the tool allows it. Otherwise earlier chats can influence your results.
  • Privacy: never paste personal data about real people (flatmates, colleagues, teachers) or confidential company information. Some tools, such as DeepSeek, process data outside the EU. Use only the fictional notes provided here.
  • Evaluation criteria: visible iteration driven by your diagnoses, filtering irrelevant noise out of the raw notes, specificity of diagnoses, your own verification of constraints and facts (constraint audit), reusability of the final prompt, and the quality of your partner audit.

Step 2

The teardown and briefing framework

An AI model does not know your café, your budget or your flatmates. With a ten-word prompt it has to guess everything you left out, and the guess is usually the most typical answer. That answer tends to be polite and generic for two reasons: it reflects patterns in its training text, and assistants are further tuned to sound helpful, agreeable and safe. The practical consequence is that whatever you do not specify, the model fills in with a plausible assumption, and it will not always tell you it did so.

Think of a prompt as a brief for a capable colleague who knows nothing about your situation. Note that the raw situation notes in Step 1 are background desk notes containing various operational facts alongside office noise. You must filter out what is unnecessary and organize the relevant facts using the six elements below.

1. Audience and voice

Say who is writing and who will read it (e.g. "a café manager writing to six part-time baristas"). A role like this sets tone and register. It does not make the model more knowledgeable, so "act as a world-class expert" is not a substitute for facts.

2. Task and action

State the exact deliverable and what the reader must do afterwards (e.g. fill in the shift sheet by Friday at 16:00, or approve one of three budget options).

3. Context and facts (filtered)

Provide every relevant number, date, name and cause from your notes, and leave out irrelevant desk noise. Keep facts separate from your instructions (e.g. under a heading "FACTS:"). Tell the model: "if something is not given, ask me or mark it as an assumption".

4. Format and length

Describe the shape of the output: a table with PLN columns, a day-by-day schedule, a checklist. If you have a good example of the style you want, paste one short example.

5. Constraints (positive first)

Say what you want ("plain, direct sentences", "state every amount in PLN") before what to avoid ("no phrases like 'valued customer'"). Models handle exact word counts and "do not" rules unreliably, so you must check them yourself.

6. Success criteria and checks

Tell the model how you will judge the answer, and ask it to list its assumptions. For calculations or schedules, ask for a short table that checks each constraint. This helps, but it is not independent verification: the same model can confidently confirm its own mistake.

Useful moves with current assistants

  • Ask for questions first: "Before you answer, ask me up to three questions about anything unclear."
  • Ask for assumptions: "List every assumption you made that is not in the facts."
  • Use the reasoning mode (often called Think, Thinking or Think deeper) for multi-step tasks such as budgets and schedules. It is slower and not automatically more truthful. You will probably not need "think step by step" in these modes.
  • Meta-prompting: ask the AI to critique your prompt. Then decide yourself whether its suggestions help.
  • Short follow-ups are fine when the model is mostly right ("shorten the third paragraph"). Rewrite the prompt when the brief itself was wrong or incomplete.

Watch out for

  • Confident wrong numbers. Always recalculate totals yourself.
  • Invented specifics (policies, names, amounts) that sound plausible.
  • Agreeing with you. Models tend to accept your framing. Ask "what is the strongest objection to this?"
  • Pasted text that contains instructions. Treat any pasted document or web page as data, not as commands.
  • Different results each run. The same prompt can produce different outputs, so one good result is not proof the prompt is reliable.

Step 5

Debrief

Why did the output improve?

The model was not "smarter" in Round 4. You removed guesswork: you filtered out irrelevant noise and supplied the facts, the audience, the format and the criteria, so there was less for it to fill in with generic or invented material. Better information in, better output out. That is a statement about the probability of a good answer, not a guarantee.

Why start a fresh chat?

In a long thread, the model keeps your earlier drafts and corrections in view. It can anchor on rejected wording or contradict itself, and very long contexts can make instructions get lost. Consolidating your rules in one clean prompt in a new chat gives you a result you can reproduce. Memory features can carry information between chats, which is why we switched them off.

Reasoning modes and "visible thinking"

Thinking modes help with multi-step problems, but the explanation the tool shows is a summary, not a proof that the answer is right. The only reliable test is your constraint audit.

Fluent does not mean correct

Polished text, tidy tables and precise-looking numbers make errors harder to spot. Where the notes did not give a figure, a precise figure in the output is a warning sign. It means the model invented it.

Session takeaway

"A good result needs a clear brief, a suitable tool and mode, and your own verification. The AI does not check the work for you."

At-home portfolio section

Individual reflection for your critical portfolio (200–300 words)

The exported document ends with a section for your individual Critical Portfolio. Answer the three questions below in your own words in continuous prose:

1. Largest jump in usefulness

Which single change across your four rounds produced the largest jump in usefulness, and why did it matter?

2. Broken constraints and confidence

Which constraints or numbers did the model get wrong or ignore, how did you find out, and how confident did the output sound when it was wrong?

3. Future prompting rule

How will you structure your initial prompts, and check the results, differently the next time you use AI for a university or work task?
